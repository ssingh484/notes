## History

### The First Crossing (Year 0)
No one planned Issyldir. Two caravans met at an unmarked desert crossing, found they had complementary goods, and decided to meet again. A seasonal camp became a permanent camp. A permanent camp became a souq. A souq became a city. The deep spring at the crossing's heart — reliable clean water in an arid region — made permanence possible.

The city's founding document is a trade agreement, not a charter. The founding symbol is a rose — adopted because a wild rose grew from the crack in the first permanent well-stone, which the founders interpreted as an auspicious sign. The rose became the city's emblem, reproduced on every civic building.

### The First Generation (Years 0–30)
The founding families established the Merchant Council and the Kafil system in the same decade. The permit system for non-native workers was presented as administrative order. It was also a mechanism for ensuring that labor could be imported without creating political competition from workers who might demand equal civic standing.

The decision to deny permanent residency access was deliberate. The archive contains the minutes of the Council meeting where it was made. The councilmember who wanted to offer residency — his name was Mas'ud al-Farhan — wrote something on a piece of paper and put it in his pocket. The paper has not been found.

### The Building Years (Years 30–90)
Issyldir became genuinely prosperous. The silk road connection made the city a mandatory stop on regional trade routes. The Bayt al-Hikma was built. The Grand Serai was built. The Hajia al-Kawthar was expanded three times. The city developed the self-consciousness of a place that knew it mattered.

The Kafil system ran silently in the background throughout this period, processing workers, denying appeals, renewing permits annually at a fee the workers had no choice but to pay. The workers built everything in the preceding paragraph.

### The Declining Years (Years 90–138)
Three generations of prosperity produced the conditions for their own disruption. Trade routes shifted. Competing cities emerged. The aquifer level began dropping — slowly, measurably, concerning. The Merchant Council's response was administrative and political rather than structural.

### Year 138–140: The Approach to the Ritual
The Amber Company's relationship with the city, 90 years old and reliable, was strained by a trade dispute that publicly embarrassed both parties. The Company offered a reconciliation package including the donated ritual components. The Council, facing water scarcity and poor trade years, accepted.

Head Scholar [[Parveen|Parveen]] objected to the ritual for six months. The Council voted 6-1 to proceed.

### Year 140: The Night of the Ritual
The Rite of the Verdant Accord was performed by the most senior scholar available — [[Parveen|Parveen]], who had objected to it. She performed it because if someone was going to attempt the corrupted ritual, she wanted to be the one closest to it, able to control the damage.

The Bloom pulse expanded from the Grand Serai outward across the entire city in approximately four hours. [[The Gardener|The Gardener]] arrived through the opened ritual tear, disoriented, alone, and accidentally welcomed by the abundance of human meaning in the space.

The city has been Bloom-integrated for 18 months.

## The Kafil System

The Kafil system is the permit-and-sponsorship framework governing non-native workers. Key elements:

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Initial permit | Required to work in the city; fee paid annually |
| Sponsor | Each worker requires a local sponsor who may revoke sponsorship at will |
| Appeal | Workers may appeal permit denial; 94% denial rate over 140 years |
| Residency | Technically available; requires Council sponsorship; never granted to a non-native in 140 years |
| Exit rule | Leaving without permit renewal voids permanent admissibility |

The exit rule is the system's cruelest element: the workers who were in the city when the Bloom occurred could not leave without permanently losing their ability to return — their income, their trade connections, everything. The Kafil system trapped people in a city that was dying.

> **READ ALOUD (first view of Issyldir)**: *"You see the city from the road before you reach its walls — or rather, you see what the city has become. Issyldir is still standing, its walls intact. The minarets are upright and the marble dome of the Grand Serai, the large capital hall and original seat of the city's power, is visible against the sky
> 
> *You have all heard of the city and its history, from conversations about its storied past, from merchants singing its praises or from travelling caravans describing the source and related prestige of their goods.*
> 
> *This city here started a century and a half ago from a chance crossing of two trade caravans in the desert. The agreement to meet again and trade again led to the slow growth of a souq and the town surrounding it over the years. A jewel of the desert, sitting astride numerous trade routes it became a flourishing, prosperous city*
> 
> *This... is not that city, this is a changed, overgrown aftermath of a violent magical ritual. It is no longer a lively trade city but it is still alive, growing, buding and flowering.*
>
> *From here, half a mile out of the city, the color is what reaches you first — a warmth of pink and red and gold rising from the walls, the rooftops, the towers, a city crowned in roses when no city should be. The scale of it takes a moment to process: not a garden here and there, not decorative planting, but the entire visible surface of Issyldir covered in something living and flowering that should not be there. And then, because the wind is coming from that direction: the smell. Sweet. Almost too sweet. The cloying, sickly sweet smell of a thousand roses in a space meant for a hundred.*
>
> *The city seems like it is waiting, you are not sure what for."*

## The City's Character

Issyldir was always a city defined by the tension between its cosmopolitan founding and its administrative restriction. Its culture was genuinely multicultural — the four-language fountain inscription, the architectural mixing of styles, the Velduri silk stalls next to the Dakhiri spice merchants. Its politics were not. The Kafil system ensured that cultural mixture never translated to political equality.

The Bloom has not resolved this tension. It has preserved it. Every record documenting the Kafil system's injustice is perfectly intact in the archive, in root growth that has preserved it better than any conservator could have. The Gardener did not choose to preserve these records. It preserved everything. The city's beauty and its ugliness, equally.

This is Issyldir: a place that looks like a dream and functions like a trap, even now, even after the apocalypse. Players exploring it will feel both things simultaneously. That is the intention.
